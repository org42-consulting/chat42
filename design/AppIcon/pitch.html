A ground-up redraw for Apple’s Liquid Glass icon system — built as separate layers the system lights itself, rather than a picture of an icon.

Direction
Appearance

The problem

The current icon is a wordmark, and wordmarks don’t survive the transition

Today’s icon sets CHAT over a boxed 42. on a pale gradient, shipped as nineteen flat PNGs in AppIcon.appiconset. Every property Liquid Glass wants to control is already baked into those pixels: the background gradient, the flat lighting, the crop. The system has nothing left to light.

Four specific things break. The lettering runs nearly edge to edge, so the squircle mask crops it. The strokes are hairline-thin at 32 px. There is one flat layer, so there is no depth for specular highlights to describe. And there is no dark or mono variant, so the icon can’t answer the four appearances the system asks for.

The redraw keeps the brand where it actually lives — the crimson, the ink, and the 42 — and gives up the one thing that cannot be made to work: setting the full name in an icon.

Two directions

One keeps the number. One plays it safe.

Both are fully compliant. They differ in how much brand they carry.

Recommended

A — The Answer

A crimson bubble carrying the 42. It is the only mark here that could belong to this app and no other — the Hitchhiker’s joke is the product’s whole name, and an icon that drops it is a chat app like any other. The numerals are drawn as geometry, not type, so nothing depends on a font.

Alternate

B — Dialogue

Two bubbles, ink behind crimson, no numerals at all. It survives to 16 px more comfortably and sidesteps Apple’s caution about text entirely. The cost is distinctiveness: it reads as a chat app rather than as Chat42.

Appearances

All four states the system renders

Follows the direction selected above. Colour is re-mapped per appearance; the geometry never moves.

Clear and Tinted are approximations. The real material — refraction, specular response, the way the shapes pick up the wallpaper behind them — is composited by the system at render time and can only be judged in Icon Composer or on a device.

The honest test

Where icons actually get looked at

Drawn at true pixel sizes. The Dock hovers around 64 px, a Finder list row gives you 16.

Direction A holds the numerals to 32 px, verified against a real rasteriser rather than by eye on a scaled vector. Below that the bubble silhouette and the crimson carry recognition, which is how the small sizes work for most icons. Direction B stays legible the whole way down.

Anatomy

Drawn as two parts, shipped as one layer

The bubble and the numerals stay separate files because they are easier to edit that way. What ships is the two of them composed into a single Liquid Glass layer — and that was a correction the compiler forced.

What compiling it changed

Drawn as two groups — bubble behind, numerals in front — the numerals almost vanished. A second glass group stacked on a dark one picks up what is beneath it and loses nearly all its contrast. Turning specular and translucency off did not recover it; putting both layers inside one group was worse, merging them into a single object and dropping the numerals entirely. Painting the numerals into one layer renders exactly as designed.

The cost is that bubble and numerals can no longer be recoloured independently per appearance — the system derives dark and mono from the one layer. Worth it for an icon you can read.

Why crimson lifts in the dark

#990000 against a near-black ground falls to roughly 2:1 — the bubble stops separating from the background and the icon reads as a floating 42. #B01414 restores the separation at the same hue.

One thing to decide

Shipping this touches the build

build.sh deliberately avoids actool so Chat42 compiles with only the Command Line Tools, not a full Xcode. An Icon Composer .icon file needs Xcode’s toolchain to compile, and adding one replaces the existing asset catalog outright.

So this is a real trade, not a drop-in: either Xcode becomes a build requirement, or the .icon is compiled once and its output committed. The artwork is ready either way — the source SVGs and assembly notes are in design/AppIcon/.
